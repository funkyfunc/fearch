# **Legal Exposure of Client-Side Search Automation: Analysis of End-User and Developer Liability Under US, EU, and UK Law**

The deployment of client-side automation tools that interact with commercial search engines occupies a precise boundary between automated web retrieval, user-directed browser navigation, and platform contract enforcement. Assessing the legal exposure of both the individual operating the utility and the open-source author distributing the source code requires an examination of anti-hacking statutes, contract formation doctrines, intellectual property regimes, and digital protection laws across the United States, the European Union, and the United Kingdom.  
The factual model at issue presents a distinct technological footprint. The software runs entirely on the user’s local workstation, issues direct HTTP/HTTPS requests that declare an honest User-Agent, and honors exclusion directives in robots.txt under RFC 9309, abstaining from direct programmatic queries to endpoints disallowed by platforms, such as Google’s persistent /search exclusion1. In parallel, the utility triggers actual search-engine result pages (SERPs) only upon affirmative user opt-in by delegating the navigation to the user’s local Google Chrome installation, either through parameter-based URL invocations or by populating an in-page input field for manual submission. The developer maintains no centralized servers, hosts no web proxies, and sells no scraped data sets.

## **Contractual Dimensions and Notice Enforceability**

The preliminary barrier erected by search engines consists of standardized terms of service intended to prohibit automated querying and data extraction. The legal viability of these contractual covenants depends on user authentication and the technical mechanics of the interaction.  
Google’s Universal Terms of Service stipulate that users may not abuse, harm, interfere with, or disrupt their systems, explicitly enumerating automated access to content in violation of machine-readable instructions, such as robots.txt files that disallow crawling or indexing, as an actionable abuse4. Furthermore, Google's search-specific policies prohibit the transmission of automated queries of any sort without prior express permission6. In comparable terms, the Microsoft Services Agreement, which governs Bing, prohibits utilizing any automated process or service, such as bots, spiders, or meta-search tools, to access or extract data from its systems8. Privacy-focused search providers such as DuckDuckGo similarly incorporate provisions within their terms restricting automated, non-personal use of their web search interfaces11.  
The enforceability of these contractual restrictions depends directly on whether an enforceable agreement is formed between the platform and the entity executing the query. When a user runs searches within a signed-in browser profile, a valid clickwrap or sign-in-wrap agreement exists13. Having registered an account and affirmatively accepted the terms, the user is bound by platform rules13.  
Conversely, when searches occur within an unauthenticated, logged-out session, platforms must rely on browsewrap theories of formation, asserting that mere navigation through a public website binds the visitor to hyperlink-accessible terms13. In *Berman v. Freedom Financial Network, LLC* (2022), the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit reiterated that an enforceable browsewrap contract requires evidence of actual knowledge or reasonably conspicuous notice sufficient to place a reasonably prudent user on inquiry notice13. Passive hyperlinks placed in website footers typically fail to establish inquiry notice for ordinary web visitors13.  
Judicial enforceability of anti-scraping covenants against parties with accounts has narrowed substantially. In *Meta Platforms, Inc. v. Bright Data Ltd.* (2024), the United States District Court for the Northern District of California granted summary judgment in favor of the defendant, holding that Bright Data did not breach Facebook and Instagram terms of use when it scraped publicly available data outside of account authentication15. Judge Edward Chen held that platform terms govern an account holder's privileged use of the service and are structured to prevent users from abusing that access15. When an entity scrapes public data without logging into an account, it does not utilize its privileged access and stands in the position of an ordinary visitor to whom adhesive terms cannot apply as a matter of basic contract law15.  
The boundaries of contract enforcement were constrained further in *X Corp. v. Bright Data Ltd.* (2024), where Judge William Alsup dismissed state-law breach of contract and tort claims arising from public web scraping17. The court ruled that adhesive terms of use cannot be wielded to exert private, copyright-like control over publicly accessible content that the platform does not own, holding that such state-law claims conflict with and are preempted by the federal Copyright Act17.

## **Primary Legal Exposure of the End User**

An individual user operating the tool locally executes two operational modes: direct background fetching of permitted paths and opt-in browser delegation of search engine result pages.

### **United States Judicial Framework**

The primary statutory vehicle for challenging unauthorized computer access in the United States is the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), codified at 18 U.S.C. § 103015. Under § 1030(a)(2), liability attaches to anyone who intentionally accesses a computer without authorization or exceeds authorized access, thereby obtaining protected information15.  
In *Van Buren v. United States* (2021), the Supreme Court adopted a gates-up or gates-down interpretation of the statute, clarifying that an individual who possesses legitimate access to a system does not exceed authorized access simply by retrieving information for an unauthorized purpose22. Following *Van Buren*, the Ninth Circuit reaffirmed in *hiQ Labs, Inc. v. LinkedIn Corp.* (2022) that accessing publicly available web data where the website owner has erected no authentication gate or password barrier does not constitute access without authorization under the CFAA15. Because search engine result pages are served to the public without requiring credentials, accessing them via a standard Chrome browser instance remains outside the scope of CFAA unauthorized access15.  
Common-law claims for trespass to chattels require a demonstration of intentional interference with the physical possession of a computer system resulting in substantial impairment of its condition, quality, or utility23. Intermittent, low-volume browser executions driven by personal user queries do not inflict measurable burden on enterprise search infrastructure23.  
Regarding breach of contract, if the user conducts searches while logged into an active personal profile (such as a Google or Microsoft account), programmatic automation of the query submission technically breaches the platform’s anti-automation rules6. However, establishing an actionable breach of contract requires proof of damages proximately caused by the breach13. Standard consumer interactions generate no compensable financial injury for the platform25. Consequently, search engine operators do not pursue individual consumers in court for personal browser automation22. Instead, platforms rely exclusively on self-help remedies, deploying CAPTCHA verification challenges, temporary IP-based rate limits, or administrative suspensions of user accounts6.

### **European Union Legal Framework**

Under European Union law, unauthorized computer access is governed by Directive 2013/40/EU on Attacks Against Information Systems. Article 3 requires Member States to criminalize the intentional access without right to the whole or any part of an information system26. Recital 17 clarifies that access without right requires the infringement of an active security measure, such as an encryption gate, password barrier, or technological access constraint26. Submitting a search query through a consumer web browser to an open web server circumvents no technical protections and cannot constitute an offense under the Directive26.  
Database rights under Directive 96/9/EC present distinct legal considerations. Article 7 establishes a *sui generis* database right empowering database makers to prevent the unauthorized extraction or re-utilisation of substantial parts of a database evaluated quantitatively or qualitatively27. In Case C-202/12 *Innoweb B.V. v. Wegener*, the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) held that a dedicated meta-search engine that queries a third-party database in real time and presents the results directly to end users performs an act of re-utilisation29. However, the CJEU subsequently circumscribed this doctrine in Case C-762/19 *CV-Online Latvia v. Melons*, ruling that an aggregator’s extraction or re-utilisation infringes the *sui generis* right only if it causes significant detriment to the database maker's substantial investment in obtaining, verifying, or presenting the content29.  
An individual user issuing personal queries in Chrome extracts insubstantial fragments of data for transient private consumption28. The user neither aggregates nor commercializes the results, avoiding infringement under Article 728. Furthermore, while the CJEU established in Case C-30/14 *Ryanair Ltd v PR Aviation BV* that database creators whose collections do not qualify for statutory *sui generis* or copyright protection may enforce standard contractual restrictions against automated access under domestic contract law, such enforcement in the consumer context remains subject to Directive 93/13/EEC on Unfair Terms in Consumer Contracts, which renders unnegotiated clauses that excessively restrict normal consumer device functionality unenforceable27.  
Data protection under Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR) does not impose compliance burdens on the end user34. While search result pages frequently display personal data (such as names, profile snippets, and professional addresses), Article 2(2)(c) of the GDPR provides an express exemption for the processing of personal data by a natural person in the course of a purely personal or household activity. An individual utilizing an automated utility on their personal computer for non-commercial information retrieval falls squarely within this household exemption.

### **United Kingdom Statutory Framework**

The United Kingdom evaluates unauthorized interaction under the Computer Misuse Act 1990 (CMA). Section 1 establishes an offense when a person causes a computer to perform any function with intent to secure access to any program or data held in any computer, knowing that the access is unauthorized35. Section 3 penalizes unauthorized acts done with intent to impair, or recklessness as to impairing, the operation of a computer or hindering access to any program or data35.  
The scope of authorization regarding internet-facing systems was defined by the High Court in *DPP v. Lennon* \[2006\] EWHC 1201 (Admin)40. The Divisional Court held that an entity connecting a computer system to the public internet confers implied consent to receive communications of the kind the system is configured to process26.  
Although *Lennon* established that implied consent is vitiated when communications are dispatched with the specific intent to overload, disrupt, or interrupt operations (as demonstrated by Lennon's transmission of millions of spoofed emails to crash an employer’s servers), ordinary HTTP and HTTPS navigation sent to a public search engine falls within the owner’s implied consent39. A user running a small-scale automation utility exhibits no intent to impair infrastructure under Section 3, and because access to the public search engine interface requires no circumvention of credentials, no violation of Section 1 arises35.

## **Secondary and Direct Legal Exposure of the Tool's Author**

Software developers who publish open-source tools that can automate interactions with third-party web platforms occupy a distinct legal position from service providers that run scraping infrastructure. The author under evaluation distributes standalone code without maintaining proxy networks, operational endpoints, or harvesting services.

### **United States Civil and Statutory Exposure**

Under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, federal courts do not recognize an independent cause of action for civil aiding and abetting (*Freeman v. DirecTV, Inc.*, 9th Cir. 2006). Because the end user’s execution of public searches does not violate the CFAA, no primary liability exists to support an accessory claim against the developer15.  
The primary battleground regarding automated query tools involves the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA), codified at 17 U.S.C. § 120142. Section 1201(a)(1) prohibits circumventing a technological protection measure (TPM) that effectively controls access to a copyrighted work, while Section 1201(a)(2) prohibits manufacturing, importing, offering to the public, or trafficking in any technology primarily designed or produced for the purpose of circumventing a TPM42.  
The application of Section 1201 to automated search retrieval was raised in *Google LLC v. SerpApi, LLC*, No. 5:25-cv-10826, filed in the Northern District of California in December 202542. Google’s complaint asserts that its search results incorporate proprietary and licensed copyrighted materials, including Knowledge Panel photographs, licensed business entries, and graphical Maps assets42. Google alleges that it deployed a specialized traffic-management and access-control measure known as "SearchGuard," which delivers dynamic JavaScript challenges and browser verification checks to prevent automated extraction42. The complaint claims SerpApi engaged in unlawful circumvention under § 1201(a)(1) and illegal trafficking under § 1201(a)(2) by deploying residential proxy networks, fabricating browser fingerprint signatures, and programmatically resolving CAPTCHA challenges to extract search data at a rate of hundreds of millions of daily queries42.  
The open-source tool at issue differs from SerpApi in architecture, method, and intent. The software incorporates no code designed to bypass or crack SearchGuard, maintains no rotating proxy routing system, and does not harvest search results via headless browser manipulation42. Direct programmatic requests generated by the tool strictly respect RFC 9309 robots.txt instructions, and all search page visits occur through the user's authentic local Google Chrome installation with complete browser integrity intact1.  
Under the doctrine articulated in *Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios, Inc.* (1984), the distribution of a commercial product or tool does not generate contributory copyright liability if the product is widely capable of substantial non-infringing uses. Browser automation utilities that open local applications and execute human-guided research represent general-purpose tools with substantial legitimate applications.  
Regarding state-law economic torts, such as intentional interference with contractual relations, a plaintiff platform must prove that the defendant intentionally induced a contracting party to breach an agreement, resulting in demonstrable damage13. Under *MGM Studios Inc. v. Grokster, Ltd.* (2005), inducement liability requires affirmative steps taken with the clear purpose of fostering unlawful acts, such as advertising an improper use or actively steering users toward breach. An author who writes an open-source utility that identifies its User-Agent honestly, respects robots.txt, and requires explicit user opt-in before opening standard browser tabs does not satisfy the intentional inducement standard23.  
Furthermore, as held in *X Corp. v. Bright Data Ltd.* (2024), state-law interference claims asserted by platforms seeking to penalize tools that access public data are preempted by federal copyright law when they attempt to create exclusive extraction monopolies over uncopyrightable or public assets17.  
In *Meta Platforms, Inc. v. BrandTotal Ltd.* (2022), Meta challenged BrandTotal’s UpVoice Chrome extension, which harvested advertising metrics while users browsed Facebook21. The court found that BrandTotal breached Meta’s terms and engaged in unauthorized access because BrandTotal operated an enterprise infrastructure that commercially monetized data extracted from authenticated user sessions21. In contrast, an open-source author who neither collects nor commercializes user data and merely publishes source code cannot be equated with an entity running a commercial scraping enterprise16.

### **European Union Legal Position**

Within the European Union, the liability of a software distributor is analyzed under general civil liability and intermediate liability principles. The tool's author does not host or transmit queries and therefore does not function as an intermediary service provider under Regulation (EU) 2022/2065 (Digital Services Act). Instead, the author is evaluated as a software developer under Member State tort frameworks.  
Civil liability for software distribution under civil law regimes (such as the German *Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch* or the French *Code civil*) requires proof of an unlawful act, intentional or negligent fault, and direct causation of harm. Distributing open-source software that automates desktop interactions constitutes a lawful activity under basic principles of technological freedom.  
Under the Database Directive (Directive 96/9/EC), liability for re-utilisation attaches to the entity that actively extracts or distributes the database content27. In *Innoweb*, liability arose because the defendant itself operated a server infrastructure that received queries, polled the *AutoTrack* engine in real time, and presented the results under its own commercial banner29. An author who merely writes a local program that directs an end user’s browser to a public search engine neither extracts nor re-utilizes a database, eliminating exposure under EU database law29.

### **United Kingdom Criminal and Tort Exposure**

The legal position of software authors in the UK requires scrutiny under Section 3A of the Computer Misuse Act 1990, introduced by Section 37 of the Police and Justice Act 200649. Section 3A creates offenses concerning articles developed or supplied for computer misuse:

> 1. Under Section 3A(1), it is an offense to make, adapt, supply, or offer to supply any article intending it to be used to commit, or assist in the commission of, an offense under Section 1, 3, or 3ZA35.  
> 2. Under Section 3A(2), a person commits an offense if they supply or offer to supply any article *believing that it is likely to be used* to commit such an offense36.

The statutory reference to an "article" encompasses software programs and electronic code49. The breadth of Section 3A(2) raised widespread concern within the UK technology and security sectors regarding the legal exposure of developers distributing dual-use utilities, such as port scanners, automated audit tools, and browser macros36.  
To calibrate enforcement and prevent the chilling of legitimate technical development, the Crown Prosecution Service (CPS) promulgated formal *Cybercrime Prosecution Guidance* regarding Section 3A35. The guidance explicitly instructs prosecutors that:

* Many programs have a dual use, possessing legitimate functions alongside potential misuse, requiring prosecutors to prove that the suspect possessed actual criminal intent before bringing charges35.  
* In evaluating Section 3A(2) prosecutions, prosecutors must determine whether the article was developed primarily, deliberately, and for the sole purpose of committing a CMA offense36.  
* Prosecutors must examine whether the article is widely used for legitimate purposes, possesses a substantial installation base, and was supplied in an ordinary commercial or open-source context36.

Because the utility under evaluation is not designed to breach computer security, contains no payload intended to impair system operations under Section 3, and respects robots.txt exclusion protocols, it does not meet the threshold of an article developed for the purpose of committing a computer misuse offense2. The CPS guidance effectively shields open-source developers who release general-purpose automation tools lacking malicious criminal intent35.  
Under English common law, economic tort exposure for inducing a breach of contract is governed by the principles established in *Lumley v Gye* and affirmed by the House of Lords in *OBG Ltd v Allan* \[2007\] UKHL 21\. Establishing liability requires that the defendant possessed actual knowledge of the contract, intended to procure its breach, and engaged in direct persuasion, inducement, or assistance that caused the breach to the claimant’s demonstrable economic loss.  
Publishing passive, general-purpose open-source code on a public forge does not constitute targeted procurement of contractual breaches23. The author lacks both the specific intent to procure breaches of search platform terms and direct causal involvement in the end user’s discretionary search queries23.

## **Synthesis and Enforcement Realities**

The application of statutory law and judicial precedent demonstrates that client-side search automation utilities of this design carry minimal legal exposure for both the end user and the open-source author.  
For the individual user, accessing publicly accessible search engine result pages through an unmodified Chrome browser session does not violate federal anti-hacking legislation in the United States, as confirmed by *Van Buren* and *hiQ Labs*15. Under United Kingdom law, the public deployment of web servers establishes implied consent for standard HTTP requests under *DPP v. Lennon*, provided the requests do not seek to impair operations39. Within the European Union, the extraction of isolated search listings does not meet the substantiality requirements of the Database Directive, and the retrieval of public data for personal inquiries is shielded by the household processing exemption under Article 2(2)(c) of the GDPR28.  
The user's exposure is almost entirely administrative. If the tool is used within an authenticated session, platform terms are breached, but the platform's response is confined to operational countermeasures: automated CAPTCHA prompts, transient IP rate limitations, and account suspensions6. Because the user inflicts no compensable economic loss, damages claims are unviable25.  
For the open-source author, exposure is insulated by the architecture of the tool. Landmark enforcement actions brought by major search engines—such as *Google LLC v. SerpApi, LLC* under DMCA § 1201—target commercial entities that systematically defeat bot-management systems (such as SearchGuard), maintain residential proxy pools, solve CAPTCHAs programmatically, and redistribute billions of harvested results for profit42.  
The tool at issue occupies the opposite posture:

* It maintains no server infrastructure and extracts no data on behalf of third parties.  
* It respects robots.txt under RFC 9309 for programmatic background fetches, avoiding disallowed endpoints1.  
* It delegates user-consented search operations to an authentic, locally installed Chrome browser, invoking no technological circumvention mechanisms42.

Under the *Sony* doctrine in the United States and the Crown Prosecution Service’s dual-use guidance under Section 3A of the UK Computer Misuse Act, the distribution of general-purpose software capable of substantial non-infringing uses does not create criminal or secondary civil liability in the absence of criminal intent35. By relying on local browser delegation, respecting machine-readable directives, and avoiding circumvention technology, the utility remains well within the boundaries of lawful software distribution.
